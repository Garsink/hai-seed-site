The Company of 2046: When AI Becomes the Business

A hypothetical morning twenty years from now, and the uncomfortable questions it raises.

Deividas Jurkštas, Founder, HAI Seed · 1 November 2026 · 4 min read

It is a Tuesday in 2046, a little after seven. Your biggest customer's AI has been talking to your company's AI since four in the morning. Their maintenance data says a component will need replacing in six weeks. Your system has already found one in stock, checked its records, confirmed the customer's credit, priced it, compared two freight options and put a reservation on the part. On the other side, their purchasing system has compared your offer with three others and is waiting for one detail about the paperwork.

Nobody at your company has opened an inbox yet.

So what exactly is the CEO supposed to do all day?

I run an aviation business, and the question is less theoretical than it sounds. A single component can come with thousands of pages of records from different owners, repair shops and formats. Piecing that history together used to take an engineer several days. In my own company it now takes around two hours, and the engineer still makes the final call. Twenty years is a long time for a gap like that to keep closing.

Here is where I think it goes, and where I might be wrong.

Will a company still need its managers?

A lot of management exists because people have to pass information to each other. Sales asks finance whether a customer can have credit. Logistics finds out in a meeting that a shipment is late. A manager's week is full of asking, chasing and repeating.

When a company's systems share one current picture, most of that traffic disappears. I expect fewer layers, and managers who spend their time on people, exceptions and judgement rather than on status updates.

Where I might be wrong: every previous wave of software was supposed to flatten organisations, and most of them grew new layers to manage the software.

Will anyone make money when everyone has the same AI?

By 2046 a capable AI will be as ordinary in an office as electricity. Every competitor will have one, and most will be very good. In some markets that will squeeze margins, because when every seller prices perfectly and answers instantly, the buyer gets most of the benefit.

A company can become far more productive without becoming more profitable. That is worth saying out loud now, before everyone promises the opposite.

So what stays valuable when intelligence is cheap? Probably what was always hard to copy: capital, stock on the shelf, approvals and licences, relationships, reputation. I would add one more to that list, because it is the one most companies neglect: their own verified knowledge. What they promised, to whom, what happened afterwards, which customers pay late but always pay, which suppliers send clean paperwork. Most companies do not keep this anywhere today. It sits in people's heads and old email, and a good part of it leaves when people do.

A company can become far more productive without becoming more profitable.

If the AI decides better, who decides?

This is the question I find hardest. For many routine decisions the AI will simply be better, faster and more consistent than a tired person on a Friday afternoon. Arguing otherwise is nostalgia.

What stays with people is the part that was always the hardest: choosing what is worth building, deciding how much risk to carry, and answering for the result when it goes wrong. I expect AI to make thousands of decisions a day without asking me. How much authority it gets, how much capital it may put at risk and when it must stop and ask: those are the owner's decisions, and so is the responsibility when something goes wrong.

What should a company do about it now?

Not much that is dramatic. Start keeping your own knowledge as if it were an asset, because it is becoming one of the few that a competitor cannot copy. Know what you promised and what happened. Keep the record with the company, in a form you can take with you whatever AI you use in 2030 or 2040.

That is the bet behind what we are building: before any AI does clever work for a company, it has to know that company, including what it got wrong last time.

I do not know exactly what 2046 looks like, and neither does anyone else. I would rather be early and partly wrong than arrive in 2035 with a company that cannot remember what it promised last month.
