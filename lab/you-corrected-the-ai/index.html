You Corrected the AI. Why Was It Wrong Again?

Two corrections, and why a good AI should treat them very differently.

HAI Seed Lab · 1 November 2026 · 3 min read

Both examples below are illustrations, not cases.

It is the third Monday in a row that the weekly brief flags the same thing: one customer makes up a large share of the company's open receivables. The owner has explained twice already. This is their biggest account, the relationship is fifteen years old, and they always pay.

The same Monday, the brief also flags a missing invoice from a supplier. That one is simply wrong. This supplier always invoices on the tenth, every month, without fail. It is the fourth.

Both look like the same kind of moment: the AI said something, the owner disagrees, the owner corrects it. Most tools handle them the same way. They should not.

Wrong, or known?

The supplier flag is a false alarm. The AI misread a normal pattern as a problem. The right response is to learn the pattern so the same mistake never comes back.

The customer flag is not a false alarm at all. The concentration is real. The owner is not saying the AI is wrong; the owner is saying we know, and we have decided to live with it. If the AI learns from that correction that this customer is never a risk, it has learned the wrong lesson. One day the account doubles, or starts paying late, and the one alert that mattered stays silent because somebody said "we know" in March.

A correction should teach an AI not to repeat a mistake. It should never teach it to stop seeing something real.

What happens in HAI Seed

The owner has two answers to any finding, and they mean different things.

"Wrong." HAI keeps the owner's words and the date, records the finding as a false alarm, and turns the correction into a check that runs every day, to prevent the same false alarm from recurring.

"Dealt with." The finding steps aside, but it stays on record as a known risk. When new evidence arrives, such as a new invoice or a materially larger amount, it comes back for another look, because the situation the owner accepted is no longer the one in front of them. Not every kind of change triggers that yet. The principle is that new evidence should.

Even "wrong" is not forever where money is concerned. If the amount at stake grows materially, the finding gets a second look, whatever was said before.

Why the difference matters

Over months, these answers turn into the company's own operating knowledge: which patterns are normal, which risks the owner has knowingly accepted, which numbers can be trusted. Kept carelessly, the same answers turn into a list of things the AI has been told to ignore.

Every tool accepts corrections. The real test comes a year later: have the corrections made it more accurate without making it blind?

A correction should teach an AI not to repeat a mistake. It should never teach it to stop seeing something real.

Does it learn, or does it just remember?

There is an uncomfortable question underneath all this. An AI that stores every correction will also store every mistaken assumption its owner ever made. "That customer always pays" is a useful correction right up to the month it stops being true.

The difference between learning and remembering is whether the system keeps checking its corrections against what actually happens. Remembering is the easy half. The harder half is noticing when a correction has quietly gone out of date, and that is the half we are working on next.
