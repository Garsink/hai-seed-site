The AI Was Running. The Work Wasn't.

What we found when we checked whether our own AI was actually doing its job.

Deividas Jurkštas, Founder, HAI Seed · 1 November 2026 · 4 min read

On one of our office computers, the AI app had a set of scheduled jobs: summaries, checks, small reports. Every one of them was switched on. The schedule was set. The screen showed nothing wrong.

In July something small changed on that computer, and nobody noticed. From that day on, every job failed at the moment it started. No error message reached anyone, because the thing that would have sent the message was the thing that had stopped. We found it in October, more than two months later, while working on something else.

This is an awkward discovery for a company that builds software meant to notice what people miss.

How a working system goes quiet

It was not a one-off. Once we started looking, we found the same pattern in other forms.

When we reorganised how our AI work was set up during the summer, some scheduled jobs simply did not come along. Others sat waiting for a confirmation click that nobody was there to give. Each of these looked fine from a distance: the computer was on, the app was installed, the jobs had names and times.

The common thread is uncomfortable. Our monitoring was asking whether things were running. It was not asking whether anything had come out the other end.

What we changed

We changed the question. Our monitoring used to ask whether each job was running. The question we are moving every job to is whether it delivered: the report exists, the summary arrived, the message went out. A system that reports it is running has proved nothing yet. A system that has to show its output every day can still fail, but it cannot fail quietly for long.

So every scheduled job is getting an expected result and a time by which it should exist, and an empty slot at that time is the alarm. We are also making the setup survive the dull failures that started all this: an app that closes, a computer that falls asleep, a job that waits for someone to click. None of it is clever. All of it is the kind of thing you only build after you have been caught out.

This is an awkward discovery for a company that builds software meant to notice what people miss.

The same lesson, three more times

What surprised me most is how many of our other lessons turned out to be versions of this one.

In August I asked whether a set of problems my team had raised were really solved. On the surface they all were. Underneath sat three separate failures, and the simplest of them was a fix that had been written and saved but never put into use. Nobody had checked. Since then, "done" means someone looked at the result in the system where it matters.

Around the same time I realised the AI had been asking me questions inside dozens of separate work sessions. I only found them by going back through the sessions one by one. The questions had been asked. They had just never arrived anywhere I would see them. Now every question that blocks work goes into one list, which reaches me twice a day.

And the messages. An AI that can send messages will send too many, and within weeks people stop reading. The one alert that matters gets buried among the ones that did not. What fixed it was rationing: each kind of message gets a daily budget, a repeat is blocked, and a job with nothing to report sends nothing.

What this changed in what we sell

All of this went into HAI Seed, which is the version of our system we install in other companies. Its routines are judged by what they produce. A brief that has not arrived by its time raises an alarm that cannot be muted. Messages are rationed. Questions that need the owner go to one place.

Keeping the app itself alive on a customer's computer is the next piece we are adding.

The aviation habit helped more than I expected. In our industry a missing record or an inconsistent date matters, and you learn to ask for the evidence rather than the reassurance. It turns out an AI system needs exactly the same treatment.
