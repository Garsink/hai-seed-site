Finance 10 resultsBills never sent, payments that went quiet and numbers that didn’t add up.
  • A forgotten month of rent, found and billed: over $90,000.

    A rental ran for nine months, but only eight invoices had gone out. HAI matched the rental dates with the invoices and the finance team’s emails, found the missing month, and the bill was sent.

    Aviation business

    What HAI connected Rental dates, invoice files, system records and finance emails.

  • $1.97 million paid upfront to 31 suppliers, traced line by line.

    HAI matched every advance with its order, its repair job and the emails around it. The result was one action list: a refund to claim back, a repeat payment to stop and a deposit to offset, each with an owner and a deadline. Five weeks later, $1.04 million of those advances had been closed in the books.

    Aviation business

    What HAI connected Accounting entries, purchase and repair orders, and the team’s emails.

  • $6.4 million of orders still waiting to be invoiced, sorted by age.

    HAI found 296 order lines not yet invoiced, worth $6.4 million, and pulled out about $3.4 million from orders older than six months, so the team could check what was holding each one up.

    Aviation business

    What HAI connected Every order line, its type and how each customer’s orders are processed.

  • $2.4 million of costs were already in the system, but missing from the profit forecast.

    On 78 sales lines, costs had been recorded but were never counted in the expected profit. HAI found the missing link and prepared a fix inside the existing system, without rebuilding anything.

    Aviation business

    What HAI connected The live system, what can be changed in it, and how the company’s two main systems connect.

  • One changed due date made the oldest debts look smaller.

    Before a weekly management meeting, HAI noticed that the oldest overdue debt, not counting the biggest debtor, had dropped from $73,370 to $37,398 with no payment: one invoice’s due date had been moved back a month. It also showed that $208,375 of the week’s money in was a customer’s advance for future orders, not collected debt. The meeting got a simple rule: new payment terms only count going forward.

    Aviation business

    What HAI connected The overdue-debt report and its change history, the week’s bank receipts and promises from earlier meetings.

  • Returned equipment might still be costing rent. HAI found it while checking 13 linked rentals.

    In one day, HAI mapped 13 arrangements where the company rented aircraft parts from suppliers and rented them on to customers, then re-checked the latest bills. Four live rentals: about $104,000 a month paid out and $148,000 a month billed. It also found a returned set with no customer, where rent could keep running until the formal return, and more than 10 places where contracts, systems and bills disagreed.

    Aviation business

    What HAI connected Supplier and customer bills, rental records, contracts and emails.

  • Months before a first audit, HAI found where the books didn’t add up.

    Checking sales, customers and costs across two systems, HAI found sales with no customer attached, one deal that looked 99% profitable but was really about 9%, and months of sales recorded without their costs after a system change. Every point went onto the managers’ task list, with the audit date.

    Aviation business

    What HAI connected Sales and customer history from accounting, plus stock and sales records from the main business system.

  • A tax calculation re-checked in one evening: a missing sale and a misleading average.

    HAI rebuilt the cost of every invoice line and checked the finance team’s tax spreadsheet against it. A large sale was missing from the total, and one average cost figure hid very different profits on different products. All ten findings were checked twice and reached the finance lead the same evening.

    Aviation business

    What HAI connected Invoice lines from the main business system, the old system’s archive, the accounting profit report and the finance team’s spreadsheet.

  • Sales grew fast. Profit didn’t. HAI showed why.

    Comparing nine months of accounts across two years, HAI found that two one-off engine deals carried most of the growth while the everyday trade shrank. The lesson for the managers: judge each deal by what the company keeps and by its worst case, not by its size.

    Aviation business

    What HAI connected Two years of accounts, invoice lines, credit notes and contracts.

  • An engine deal checked before signing: the model showed losses.

    HAI went back through a year of buyer emails, engine records and inspection reports. The prices being repeated among the partners turned out to be the company’s own asking prices, not buyers’ offers, and the only written bid was more than a year old. Three separate checks estimated millions in losses under the proposed terms, so a route needing no company cash was proposed instead.

    Aviation business

    What HAI connected Buyer emails, each engine’s paperwork and inspection reports, partner chats and market prices.
